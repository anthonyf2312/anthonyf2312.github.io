RUSH ROYALE · BASICS

How a
match works

Rush Royale is a tower defence game where you do not choose which tower you get. That one rule explains almost everything else about it.

Last reviewed 2026-08-29 · Helped with Claude Code

The board

You get a 3×5 grid — fifteen tiles. Your opponent has an identical one. Enemies walk a lane past your board and you kill them before they get through.

Fifteen tiles sounds like a lot until you realise you cannot choose what lands on them. Board space is the resource the whole game is really about.

Mana

You start every match with 100 mana, and earn more by killing enemies. Mana buys two things:

  • Summoning — putting a new unit on a free tile.
  • Upgrading — raising your units' power during the match.

Summons get more expensive as you place more of them, so mana pressure builds across a match rather than easing off.

How you spend mana depends entirely on the deck. Some decks want units down early and are happy to commit; others generate mana themselves and can afford a pace that would bankrupt a different five cards. There is no universal rule here — the deck sets it.

Your deck

A deck is exactly five cards. That is the entire deckbuilding decision — and it is a big one, because every unit you summon is drawn at random from those five.

So a five-card deck is really a statement about probability. Put a card in your deck and roughly one in five summons will be it. Put a card in that only works under a condition you cannot arrange, and one in five of your summons is wasted.

Summoning

You do not pick which unit appears. You pay mana, and one of your five cards lands on a free tile at random.

Because you cannot choose, a deck is built as a set of roles rather than as a pile of good cards. The norm is one damage unit — the carry — and four cards whose job is to make it hit harder. Those four are not filler; they are where most of the damage actually comes from.

RoleWhat it is there to do
DamageThe carry. Does the killing, and in PvP it is the unit whose hit on the Phantom gets converted into your opponent's boss.
SupportRaises the carry's damage or attack speed, or generates extra mana so you can field more of it.
DebuffWeakens or slows what is walking at you, buying the carry time to kill it.
Special / SummonEverything that does not fit the three above — board manipulation, extra bodies, effects built for one strategy.

So a card is not judged on its own strength. It is judged on what it does for the other four — which is why a card that cannot help the carry is a card wasting one summon in five. You can filter the roster by role on the units page.

Merging

Drag one unit onto an identical unit of the same rank and they merge: the two become one unit of the next rank up, drawn at random from your deck — not necessarily the unit you merged.

Merging does two things at once, and only one of them is good:

  • It frees a tile. Good — you have somewhere to summon.
  • It removes a body. Bad — two units were shooting, now one is.

Merging is not automatically an upgrade. Five units at a low rank often out-damage two units at a high one, because five things are firing instead of two. Merge because you need the tile or need the rank for a specific wave — not because the board looks cluttered.

Board rank (raised by merging, resets every match) is a different thing from card level (bought with gold outside a match, permanent). See progression.

Waves, the Phantom and bosses

Enemies arrive continuously along the lane. There are three kinds of them, and they behave differently enough that it is worth knowing which one is walking at you.

MonsterHealthSpeedDamage to your gate
Commonbaseline, and grows every 10 seconds in PvPnormal1
Fasthalf a common'stwice as fast1
Mini bossfive times a common'sslightly slower2

Mini bosses also give five times the mana a common or fast monster does, so they are worth killing rather than merely surviving.

The Phantom PvP only

At the end of each round a Phantom appears and your units attack it. The damage you deal to it is converted into the health of the boss that appears on your opponent's path. Your kills already send them traffic; the Phantom is how you send them the thing that actually ends matches.

How much of that damage carries over depends on the wave number. During the first waves the amount of health converted is insignificant, and it starts to grow from wave 3 onwards.

That is the single most useful thing to know as a beginner. Early waves are close to free: you can spend them building the board instead of racing to deal damage, and decks that need time to come together get that time. The game is decided in the second half.

The more damage a player inflicts on the Phantom, the stronger the boss that appears on the opponent's path when the Phantom turns into it. The first player to kill their boss gains a bonus to all mana received, lasting until the end of the battle — and these bonuses stack, so winning the race repeatedly compounds.

A boss fight ends when the boss dies or reaches your gate; either way the next wave starts, provided both gates still have lives. Bosses and mini bosses take 2 hearts.

Which bosses

Early PvP cycles through six: Tamer, Gorgon, Bedlam, Warlock, Tribunal and Puppeteer. Higher arenas add Virus (from Arena 7) and Assassin (from Arena 9). King Pudding and Grandmaster are in the game too, at arenas the source does not record.

They are not equally dangerous. Tamer is the mildest — it summons commons behind itself, and the only real risk is a monster tanking alongside it. Gorgon petrifies two of your units so they stop attacking. Bedlam rerolls every unit on your board into a random one, which is the most disruptive thing any boss does.

In co-op, bosses are fixed

There is no Phantom in co-op, so nothing is being sent to anyone. A floor's bosses are part of the floor itself and their health does not depend on how another player is doing. See co-op.

Hearts, and how you lose

This works differently in the two modes, and the co-op version is far harsher than people expect coming from PvP.

In PvP: three hearts

You start with 3 hearts. That is the whole health bar.

What reaches your gateCosts youSo you can afford
An ordinary monster1 hearttwo leaks, and the third kills you
A boss2 heartsone leak — a second ends the match

Three common monsters getting through is a loss. One boss plus one common is a loss. There is no healing back to full, which is why letting even a single enemy slip past matters more than it looks like it should.

In co-op: one shared gate, no second chances

Co-op does not give each player a gate. There is one gate between you, and there are no hearts to spend:

If a single monster reaches the gate, the game is over. Not a heart lost — the whole run, immediately.

So co-op is sudden death. Everything that leaks is fatal, which makes coverage — making sure nothing gets past at all — worth more than it is in PvP, where you have two mistakes in hand.

How the PvP loop fits together

Put the Phantom next to the hearts and PvP makes sense:

  1. Monsters walk at you continuously. Anything that reaches your gate costs a heart.
  2. Every monster you kill — except a boss — reappears out of your opponent's portal as a common monster.
  3. At the end of the round the Phantom appears and your units hit it. A share of that damage — larger the later the wave — becomes the health of a boss on your opponent's path.
  4. Their boss is your doing, and yours is theirs. Whoever kills their boss first takes a stacking mana bonus for the rest of the match.
  5. If you cannot kill yours, it reaches your gate and takes 2 of your 3 hearts.

So there are two separate jobs on one board. Clearing your own waves keeps you alive; hitting the Phantom hard is what actually threatens the other player. Your damage output at the end of a wave is your attack. And if both gates are breached at the same moment, the winner is whoever has the lower total monster health left on their path.

None of this applies in co-op, where there is no Phantom and no opponent to feed — damage there has nowhere to go except the wave in front of you.

Two early mistakes

Merging to tidy up

A full board is a working board. Merge for a reason, not for neatness.

Packing the deck with damage units

The most common deckbuilding mistake, and it feels like the safe one. The norm is one damage unit supported by four other cards — see summoning and a first deck.

A first deck

There is a standard shape, and it is much narrower than it looks:

One damage unit. Four cards that make it hit harder.

That one damage unit is your carry — the unit that actually kills things, and in PvP the unit whose hit on the Phantom decides how big a boss your opponent has to survive. The other four slots are not there to add damage of their own. They are there to multiply the carry's.

What the four support slots do

JobExamples
Raise the carry's damageKnight Statue, Kobold
Raise its attack speedEnchanted Swords
Buff whatever sits near itunits with an area-of-effect buff
Keep the wave off youa slow or a control unit

Adding a second damage unit feels productive and is usually a downgrade: the two split your summons and your support, so instead of one carry that melts a boss you get two that cannot. Remember that summons are random — a five-card deck means each card is roughly one summon in five, so every slot you spend on a second carry is a slot not spent feeding the first.
