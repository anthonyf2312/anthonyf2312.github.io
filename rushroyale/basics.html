RUSH ROYALE · BASICS

How a
match works

Rush Royale is a tower defence game where you do not choose which tower you get. That one rule explains almost everything else about it.

The board

You get a 3×5 grid — fifteen tiles. Your opponent has an identical one. Enemies walk a lane past your board and you kill them before they get through.

Fifteen tiles sounds like a lot until you realise you cannot choose what lands on them. Board space is the resource the whole game is really about.

Mana

You start every match with 100 mana, and earn more by killing enemies. Mana buys two things:

  • Summoning — putting a new unit on a free tile.
  • Upgrading — raising your units' power during the match.

Summons get more expensive as you place more of them, so mana pressure builds across a match rather than easing off.

The most common beginner mistake is spending everything in the first few waves. Two or three units handle the opening comfortably. Mana saved early is mana you still have when the waves stop being easy.

Your deck

A deck is exactly five cards. That is the entire deckbuilding decision — and it is a big one, because every unit you summon is drawn at random from those five.

So a five-card deck is really a statement about probability. Put a card in your deck and roughly one in five summons will be it. Put a card in that only works under a condition you cannot arrange, and one in five of your summons is wasted.

Summoning

You do not pick which unit appears. You pay mana, and one of your five cards lands on a free tile at random.

This is why decks in Rush Royale are built around everything working rather than around one strong card. A deck with four excellent cards and one dead one is a deck that does nothing 20% of the time.

Merging

Drag one unit onto an identical unit of the same rank and they merge: the two become one unit of the next rank up, drawn at random from your deck — not necessarily the unit you merged.

Merging does two things at once, and only one of them is good:

  • It frees a tile. Good — you have somewhere to summon.
  • It removes a body. Bad — two units were shooting, now one is.

Merging is not automatically an upgrade. Five units at a low rank often out-damage two units at a high one, because five things are firing instead of two. Merge because you need the tile or need the rank for a specific wave — not because the board looks cluttered.

Board rank (raised by merging, resets every match) is a different thing from card level (bought with gold outside a match, permanent). See progression.

Waves, the Phantom and bosses

Enemies arrive in waves. What exactly spawns in each one is not written up here yet — but the part that decides PvP matches is what happens between them.

The Phantom

At the end of every wave — not some of them — the Phantom appears on your board. Your units attack it, and this is the moment the whole match turns on:

The damage you deal to the Phantom is sent to your opponent. It becomes the health of the boss that spawns on their side.

Deal 5 billion damage to the Phantom and the boss they have to kill — Bedlam, say — arrives with 5 billion health. Their damage comes back at you the same way.

A boss that reaches the gate costs 2 of a player's 3 hearts, so this is not chip damage — it is most of a life.

Why this changes everything

It means PvP is not a survival game with a scoreboard — your damage output is your attack. Clearing your own waves keeps you alive; hitting the Phantom hard is what actually threatens the other player.

Two consequences worth internalising early:

  • Peak damage matters more than steady damage. You are being measured on one number, at one moment, at the end of a wave — not on how tidily you handled the wave itself.
  • This is why decks run a single damage unit. Everything else exists to make that one unit's hit on the Phantom as large as possible. See building a deck.

Corrected by a player after the first draft of this page got it wrong. The earlier version described a fixed "miniboss every 5 waves, boss every 10" cadence, which is not how the game works. The Phantom is every wave.

Hearts, and how you lose

You start with 3 hearts. That is the whole health bar, and it is far tighter than new players expect.

What reaches the gateCosts youSo you can afford
An ordinary monster1 hearttwo leaks, and the third kills you
A boss2 heartsone leak — a second ends the match

Three common monsters getting through is a loss. One boss plus one common is a loss. There is no healing back to full and no margin to play with, which is why letting even a single enemy slip past matters far more than it looks like it should.

How the whole loop fits together

Put the Phantom next to the hearts and PvP makes sense:

  1. You clear a wave. Anything you miss costs you a heart.
  2. The Phantom appears and your units hit it.
  3. That damage becomes the health of a boss on your opponent's board.
  4. If they cannot kill it, it reaches their gate and takes 2 of their 3 hearts.

So you win by sending a boss too big for the other player to handle — and one that lands is very nearly fatal on its own. Their damage does the same to you.

In co-op you and a teammate face AI waves instead, surviving together for as long as you can rather than pressuring an opponent.

Four early mistakes

Spending all your mana in waves 1–5

The opening waves are survivable with very little. Save it.

Merging to tidy up

A full board is a working board. Merge for a reason, not for neatness.

Packing the deck with damage units

The most common deckbuilding mistake, and it feels like the safe one. The norm is one damage unit supported by four other cards — see a first deck.

Chasing rarity

Legendary does not mean strong — it means rare. Four Common units that support each other beat one expensive Legendary that has nothing to work with. See what rarity actually changes.

A first deck

There is a standard shape, and it is much narrower than it looks:

One damage unit. Four cards that make it hit harder.

That one damage unit is your carry — the unit that actually kills things and, more importantly, the unit that dumps damage into the Phantom at the end of a wave. The other four slots are not there to add damage of their own. They are there to multiply the carry's.

What the four support slots do

JobExamples
Raise the carry's damageKnight Statue, Kobold
Raise its attack speedEnchanted Swords
Buff whatever is near itBanner, Grindstone
Keep the wave off youa slow or a control unit

Adding a second damage unit feels productive and is usually a downgrade: the two split your summons and your support, so instead of one enormous hit on the Phantom you get two mediocre ones. Remember that summons are random — a five-card deck means each card is roughly one summon in five, so every slot you spend on a second carry is a slot not spent feeding the first.
