What a unit is

A unit is a card you put in your five-card deck. During a match you cannot summon it on demand — you pay mana and one of your five cards appears at random. So a unit is less "a tower you place" and more "one of five things that might show up".

Each unit has a rank on the board, raised by merging, and a level on the card itself, raised permanently with gold outside matches. Two different numbers, easy to confuse.

What rarity actually means

There are four tiers: Common, Rare, Epic and Legendary. Rarity does not mean "better". It controls three things:

Rarity controlsWhat that means in practice
Which chests it appears inRarer cards show up less often, so duplicates are slower to collect
How often the shop offers itYou see rare cards for sale less
What upgrades costRarer cards cost more gold per level — and legendaries additionally need magic orbs

A Legendary you have one copy of is worse than a Common you have two hundred copies of, because the Common is actually levelled. Rarity is a measure of supply, not of strength.

The four roles

Damage

Kills things, and dumps damage into the Phantom at the end of a wave — which is what actually pressures your opponent. A deck normally runs exactly one of these, called the carry. Damage units do not help each other, so a second one splits your summons and your support instead of doubling your output.

Support

Deals little or no damage itself and makes the carry better. This is where four of your five slots go. Knight Statue and Kobold raise damage; Enchanted Swords raises attack speed; Banner and Grindstone buff whatever is near them; Earth Elemental makes poison from any source hurt more. Support is not the supporting cast — in this game it is most of the deck.

Control

Slows, chills or otherwise delays enemies so your damage has longer to work. Cold Mage is the plainest example. Control looks weak on paper because it kills nothing, and is what keeps you alive on the waves that would otherwise overrun you.

Summoner

Puts extra units on the board without you paying mana for them. The Summoner itself, on merging, drops a random unit from your deck onto a free tile at a rank between 1 and its own merge rank.

Damage types

Damage type matters mostly because units that share one amplify each other.

Physical

Direct hits. Usually built around attack speed, critical hits, or clearing groups.

Magic

Elemental effects that often carry something extra — Lightning Mage chains between enemies, Fire Mage leaves a burn, Cold Elemental makes what it hits take more damage with each icy shard and hits harder still into enemies already stunned or slowed.

Poison

Damage over time rather than on impact. Alchemist drops short-lived poison puddles on random spots in the lane every few seconds; Earth Elemental infects enemies so poison from any source does more. Poison rewards commitment: one poison card is mediocre, and three that feed each other are not.

One carry, four supports

The standard deck shape is one damage unit and four cards that multiply it. Not two damage units and three supports, and definitely not four damage units — one.

The reason sits in the Phantom: at the end of each wave your damage is converted directly into the health of the boss your opponent must kill. You are being scored on one big number at one moment, so concentrating everything into a single unit beats spreading it across several.

So the question to ask of a candidate card is not "is this card good?" but "what does this do for my carry?" Raise its damage (Knight Statue, Kobold), raise its attack speed (Enchanted Swords), buff whatever sits beside it (Banner, Grindstone), or keep the wave off it. A card that does none of those is a bad card for this deck, whatever colour its border is.

Placement

Most units do not care where they land. The ones that do are support units with an area of effect: Banner and Grindstone help the units around them, so dropping one in a corner wastes most of it. When one of those appears in a bad spot, that is a genuine reason to merge.
